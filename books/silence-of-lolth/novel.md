# **The Silence of Lolth**

# Chapter 1

The temple breathed in ticks. Not the lazy domestic rhythm of a water-clock or the pompous chime of a clepsydra — this was something older, something alive. Hundreds of movements inhaling and exhaling at once, each wheel a lung, each lever a heartbeat. K'liira knew the sound better than her own breathing because she had spent more hours listening to it than she had spending speaking to anyone.

She knelt on the obsidian floor, her knees pressed into stone so cold it burned through the thin linen of her novice robes. The cavern stretched above her in darkness, vaulted ceilings lost somewhere beyond the reach of her candle-flame. Blood-stained silk hung from the upper galleries in tattered banners, each one the colour of rust or dried wine, caught in the faint draft that moved through the temple like a slow exhalation. The air smelled of iron and ozone, the particular combination that meant someone had bled here recently, or would bleed soon. K'liira could not tell which from the scent alone.

Her palms rested flat against the stone between her feet. She pressed her forehead down until it touched the cool surface and held herself there, counting the ticks of the temple's machinery beneath the floor. Eighteen years of training had taught her the posture of devotion. Nineteen years of practice had taught her how to empty her mind while doing it. But emptiness was not the same as silence, and tonight the silence felt heavier than it had before.

The Spider Queen did not answer her prayers anymore.

It had been three months since the last time K'liira heard her goddess's voice — not the distant, echoing rumble that came through the temple's acoustics during the great rituals, but the intimate whisper that used to come in dreams, like a mother's voice from the next room. Three months of kneeling on this same stone, pressing her forehead to the same cold surface, waiting for something that never came. The other novices whispered that the silence was a sign of favour, that Lolth reserved her deepest communications for those most worthy. Vesryn had said it aloud during evening prayers yesterday, her sharp little voice cutting through the incense smoke like a blade. *Lolth speaks only to those who deserve to hear her.*

K'liira had said nothing. She never said anything when Vesryn spoke like that. She simply lowered her head further and counted the ticks.

A draft moved through the temple, carrying with it the faintest trace of something that was not iron or ozone. Something sour and warm, like old sweat and broken skin. K'liira lifted her head slowly, keeping her body still, keeping her breathing measured. The draft came from below — from the lower galleries, where the slave quarters lay behind thick walls of carved basalt. The temple was built over the slave levels by design, K'liira knew. The matrons liked the symbolism of it, the idea that the divine occupied the highest space while the worthless creatures grovelled beneath. She had read the theological justification in the sacred texts. She had memorised it. She had never believed it.

The sour warmth grew stronger. It was accompanied by something else — a sound so faint that K'liira almost missed it, almost dismissed it as the settling of stone or the dripping of condensation from the cavern ceiling. But it was not either of those things. It was a voice.

Not speaking. Not exactly. Rather like the remnants of speech, the shape of words left behind when the mouth no longer had the strength to form them. The voice was wet, bubbling through a ruined throat—a slave's final, gasping plea. A prayer. Or something that resembled one.

K'liira closed her eyes and pressed her palms harder against the stone. Her training told her to ignore it. The slave quarters were not part of the temple proper. The prayers of slaves were not part of the liturgy. The sacred texts were clear on this point: only the prayers of free drow held any spiritual weight. The prayers of slaves were noise, the babbling of creatures who lacked the capacity for true devotion. K'liira had repeated this doctrine a hundred times. She had written it in her practice exercises. She had nodded along when Vesryn quoted it back to her with that mocking tilt of her head.

The voice came again, louder now, and K'liira felt something shift inside her chest. It was not conviction. It was not rebellion. For the first time in three months, she stopped listening to the void where Lolth used to be and followed the sound of a dying thing. It was simply the recognition of a sound that demanded attention, sharp and insistent as a dying creature's cry roused from any living thing that could hear it. She could not turn away from it, just as she had turned away from her own prayers.

She rose to her feet and moved toward the edge of the gallery, her bare feet finding the familiar grooves in the obsidian floor. The stairs descended in a spiral, each step carved with the eight-legged symbol of Lolth, each tread worn smooth by centuries of novice feet. K'liira counted them as she went down, the way she had been taught. One hundred and seven steps from the main gallery to the lower antechamber. Two hundred and fourteen to the slave level. She had climbed them every evening for three years. She had never paused on the descent. Tonight she paused anyway.

The antechamber opened into a corridor lined with pillars of polished basalt. The walls here were warmer than the temple above, radiating a faint thermal pulse that K'liira could feel against her skin like the breath of a sleeping animal. Fungal lanterns hung from the ceiling in iron chains, their pale blue light casting distorted reflections across the polished stone. K'liira moved through the corridor with the careful silence of someone who knew she was trespassing, though the temple was supposed to belong to everyone who lived in it.

At the bottom of the stairs, the corridor split. The left passage led deeper into the temple's service areas — kitchens, storage rooms, the ritual bath. The right passage led to the slave quarters. K'liira took the right one.

The sour warmth became a stench. It was the smell of unwashed bodies and stale air, of wounds that had festered because no healer had time to tend them. It was the smell of fear, which had a particular quality in the deep places — metallic and sharp, like the taste of blood on the tongue. K'liira's throat tightened. She pressed her lips together and kept walking.

The slave quarters occupied a wide cavern that had been carved out specifically for that purpose. Rows of narrow sleeping niches lined the walls, each one barely large enough for a single body. Most of them were occupied. Some of them were not. K'liira moved between the rows, her candle held low, her eyes scanning the faces in the shadows. She told herself she was looking for the source of the sound, the voice. She told herself this because telling herself something made it easier to believe.

She found the voice in the third row from the far wall. A male slave, young — perhaps fifteen by human reckoning, though age was difficult to measure in the deep places where sunlight never reached. He lay on his back in a narrow niche, his body thin and pale, his skin marked with the whip-scars that all slaves bore somewhere on their bodies. His breathing was shallow and irregular, each inhalation a struggle against something that pulled at his lungs from the inside. His eyes were open but unfocused, staring at something K'liira could not see.

His mouth moved. The words came out as a whisper so faint that K'liira had to lean close to hear them, close enough to feel the heat of his breath against her cheek.

*"Mother, if you are listening —"*

He stopped. His chest convulsed. He tried again.

"I did not mean to steal the bread. I was hungry. The children were hungry too. I beg you, Matron, I am sorry. I will be good. I promise I will be good."

The mantra fractured, spiraling inward until it dissolved into a hollow echo. *I will be good. I swear it. I will be good.* Each iteration grew fainter, drained of conviction, as if the spirit behind the voice were evaporating, leaving nothing but thin air where substance once stood.

K'liira felt her hands tremble. She pressed them flat against her thighs and forced them still. Her training told her to leave. The prayers of slaves were not part of the liturgy. The prayers of slaves were noise. The prayers of slaves were irrelevant.

But the voice was dying, and K'liira could not leave while it died.

She knelt beside the niche, her robes pooling around her on the cold stone floor. She reached out and touched the boy's hand. His skin was hot against hers, fever-hot, the kind of heat that meant his body was fighting something it could not win. His fingers curled weakly around hers, gripping with the last of his strength.

*"Mother,"* he whispered. *"Please."*

And then the silence came.

Not the silence of the temple. Not the silence of Lolth's absence. This was a different kind of silence — the final, absolute silence of a breath that would not come again. The boy's chest stopped moving. His eyes remained open, staring at something K'liira could not see, but the light behind them had gone out.

K'liira stayed kneeling beside him for a long time. Her hand still held his. Her forehead still pressed against the cold stone of the floor. The temple above her continued to breathe in ticks, its gears turning, its mechanisms grinding forward, indifferent to the death that had just occurred beneath it.

She thought about Lolth. She thought about the silence that had filled her own prayers for three months. She thought about the difference between a goddess who chose not to speak and a goddess who could not.

When she finally rose, her legs felt stiff and uncooperative, as though they had forgotten how to support her weight. She looked down at the boy's face one more time. His eyes were still open. They were still empty.

K'liira turned and walked back through the corridors, past the fungal lanterns, past the basalt pillars, up the one hundred and seven steps, up the one hundred and four more. The temple's ticks sounded different on the ascent than they had on the descent. They sounded heavier. They sounded like judgment.

She returned to her position on the obsidian floor and knelt once more, pressing her forehead to the same cold surface, placing her palms in the same position between her feet. She waited for the silence to settle over her the way it always did. She waited for the emptiness to return.

But the silence felt different now. It felt like a question.

And for the first time in three months, K'liira was afraid of the answer.

A shadow moved in the corner of her vision — not a physical shadow, but a disturbance in the air, a ripple in the thermal patterns that K'liira had learned to read. She turned her head slowly and saw something she could not name, something that existed just at the edge of perception, like a memory of a face rather than a face itself. It hovered there for a moment, watching her with something that might have been compassion, might have been sorrow, might have been neither.

Then it was gone.

K'liira did not mention it to anyone. She did not record it in her practice journals. She did not tell Vesryn, who would have called it weakness. She did not tell the other novices, who would have called it delusion. She kept it the way she kept the boy's hand in hers — silently, privately, without explanation.

The temple breathed in ticks. The silence held. And K'liira knelt, listening to both.


# Chapter 2

The water in the basin was black and still, reflecting nothing but the faint glow of the fungi-lamps high above. K’liira dipped her hands into it, the cold biting deep into her bones, a sharp, grounding pain. She rubbed the lather of crushed herbs and ash over her arms, her fingers moving with the mechanical precision drilled into her since she was six cycles old. Wash. Scrub. Rinse. The routine was a prayer in itself, a way to scrub away the lingering warmth of the slave quarters, the smell of the boy’s fear that still clung to her sleeve like a second skin.

She stood before the great mirror—a sheet of polished obsidian, three times her height, set into the curved wall of the chamber. The stone was cold enough to leech the heat from her reflection, distorting her features into something alien. Her eyes, usually a vivid violet, appeared dull and shadowed in the dim light. Her hair, black as the void between stars, hung in a severe braid down her back. She looked like a statue of Lolth herself, if the goddess had ever looked so hollow.

K’liira inspected her neck. The ritual collar lay on a pedestal of basalt nearby, a circle of dark iron etched with runes that hurt the eyes if stared at too long. She picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, cold and dense. She fastened it around her throat, the metal clicking shut with a sound like a breaking bone. The runes flared faintly, a pulse of dark red heat against her skin. It was a good sign. Or a bad one. In the temple, signs were rarely clear until they were too late.

She turned to the preparation table. Three vials of incense stood in a row: myrrh for obedience, saltpeter for punishment, and powdered bone for silence. She checked the seals. Intact. She ran a finger along the edge of her ritual dagger. The blade was obsidian, sharp enough to shave hair, curved like a spider’s leg. It felt light in her hand, balanced perfectly. She liked the balance. It made her feel in control, even when she felt nothing else.

"You hold it too tightly."

The voice came from behind her, low and smooth, like oil poured over glass. K’liira did not jump. She had heard the shift in the air pressure, the subtle displacement of cold air that signaled another body entering the room. She kept her eyes on the mirror, watching Vesryn’s reflection step out of the shadows.

Vesryn leaned against the far wall, her arms crossed over her chest. She wore the white-blonde hair of House Do’Urden’s elite braided into a complex crown that twisted around her head like a nest of snakes. Her eyes, grey as storm clouds, were fixed on K’liira’s hands. On the dagger.

"I hold it as the Matron instructed," K'liira said. She set the dagger down and picked up the vial of myrrh. "With respect."

Vesryn pushed off the wall and glided closer. She moved with a predator’s grace, silent on the stone floor. "Respect," she repeated, tasting the word like it was sour milk. "Is that what we’re calling it now? You’ve been quiet for three months, K’liira. Silent as the grave. The other novices whisper that Lolth has abandoned you. That your silence is not devotion, but rejection."

K'liira felt a flicker of heat rise in her chest, a spike of anger she quickly suppressed. She uncorked the myrrh vial and poured a pinch into the bronze censer. The resin crunched under her fingers. She did not answer. Rather, she set the vial down with deliberate care, aligned its cap with the rim until it sat flush, and pressed her palms flat against the cold stone of the preparation table—still, silent, letting the weight of her composure fill the space where her response should have been.

"Is it?" Vesryn stepped into K’liira’s personal space. She smelled of lavender and iron, a cloying sweetness that masked the underlying scent of blood. "Then why do your hands shake?"

K’liira looked down. Her fingers were indeed trembling, just slightly, as she sprinkled the myrrh into the coals. The flame caught, blooming blue and bright. She clenched her fist to stop the shaking. "The cold," she said. "The chamber is cold."

Vesryn smiled. It was a sharp, mocking expression that didn’t reach her eyes. "The chamber is always cold, little sister. You’ll have to do better than that to excuse yourself." She reached out and touched the ritual collar at K’liira’s throat. Her fingers were warm, uncomfortably so. "Be careful what you pray for today. The Morning Ritual is not just about obedience. It’s about proof. And you…" She leaned in, her breath hot against K’liira’s ear. "…you have nothing left to prove."

Vesryn pulled back and turned away, her robes swirling around her ankles. "I’ll see you in the sanctum. Try not to disappoint the Matron. Again."

K’liira watched her go, watching her reflection shrink into the shadows of the doorway. Only when the door hissed shut did she allow herself to exhale. Her hand went to her throat, fingers tracing the cold iron of the collar. Vesryn was right about one thing. The cold didn’t make her hands shake. Fear did.

She looked back at the mirror. Her reflection stared back, wide-eyed and pale. But beneath the pallor, she could see it—the faint, pulsing heat signature of her own anxiety, radiating from her core like a dying star. It was ugly. It was human. And it was entirely unsuitable for a priestess of Lolth.

K’liira picked up the censer. The smoke curled up, twisting into shapes that looked like spiders, then dissipated into nothing. She blew out the flame, plunging the chamber into near-darkness, save for the faint glow of the fungi-lamps. She had to go. The sanctum awaited. And with it, the eyes of the entire House.

She stepped out of the Mirror Chamber, leaving the silence behind her, walking toward the sound of ticking gears and the heavier, deeper silence of the goddess.

The Great Sanctum smelled of hot metal and dried blood. K'liira stood at the edge of the assembled novices, her bare feet pressing into the warm obsidian floor, feeling the residual heat from yesterday's sacrifices still radiating upward through the stone. Around her, forty girls knelt in rows, their heads bowed, their breaths shallow and synchronized. Above them all, on a dais carved from a single slab of black basalt, sat Matron Malice.

She wore robes of spider-silk the color of old bruises, and her face was a mask of perfect indifference. Her black eyes moved across the assembly like a predator scanning a field for the weakest prey. K'liira kept her gaze lowered, counting the ticks of the temple's mechanisms beneath the silence. One. Two. Three. The numbers grounded her the way the girl's dying prayer had grounded her in the Mirror Chamber — not with comfort, but with purpose.

The morning rites began with the chanting. Low, guttural syllables rolled through the sanctum like the rumble of distant thunder. K'liira joined her voice to the others, matching pitch and rhythm, but her attention drifted to the woman on the dais. Matron Malice had not spoken since K'liira entered. She had not needed to. Her presence was punishment enough.

When the chanting ended, the silence that followed was heavier than the noise. It pressed against K'liira's eardrums, thick and suffocating. She felt the familiar hollow space behind her ribs where the goddess's voice should have been — three months of emptiness, three months of pretending that silence was devotion.

A novice to her left shifted uncomfortably. K'liira heard the rustle of fabric, the soft click of teeth against lips. Fear. Everyone was afraid today. She could smell it — sour sweat beneath the incense, the metallic tang of adrenaline cutting through the myrrh smoke.

Then Vesryn spoke.

Her voice cut through the sanctum like a blade drawn from a sheath — clean, sharp, precise. "Matron," she said, and the way she said it, with perfect deference wrapped around something poison-edged, made K'liira's stomach tighten. "I have a question regarding Sister K'liira's recent practices."

Forty heads turned. K'liira felt the weight of their gazes like physical pressure on her shoulders. She kept her eyes on the obsidian floor, on her own distorted reflection swimming in the polished stone. Vesryn stood three places down, her white-blonde hair catching the fungi-light like spun silver, her grey eyes fixed on K'liira with the cold calculation of someone reading a map she intended to burn.

Matron Malice's expression did not change. "Speak."

Vesryn's lips curved into something that wasn't quite a smile. "For three cycles, K'liira has performed the evening prayers alone, in the lower passages, away from the assembled order. She has not attended the communal vigils. She has not shared her visions with the senior acolytes. And when questioned, she claims the goddess speaks to her directly, without intermediary."

A murmur rippled through the novices. K'liira felt it move through them like wind through tall grass — a ripple of unease, of suspicion, of the quiet hunger that made drow children turn on each other before they learned to walk.

"She is nineteen cycles old," Vesryn continued, her voice carrying easily now that she had the audience. "She has served three years at the temple. In three years, she has never once received a vision during the communal rites. But alone? In the dark?" She let the word hang, heavy with implication. "I wonder what kind of conversation happens in the dark that cannot happen in the light."

K'liira's hands clenched at her sides. She could feel the ritual collar against her throat — dark iron, etched with runes that pulsed faintly whenever her heart beat too fast. It was flaring now. She could feel the heat of it, a dull red warmth against her skin, broadcasting her discomfort to anyone watching closely enough. Vesryn was watching closely enough.

K'liira forced her voice steady. "Matron, I serve the goddess as I have always served her. My prayers are no different from any other novice's."

Vesryn's smile sharpened. "Are they? Because I have noticed something else. During the preparation for the morning rites — yesterday, in the Mirror Chamber — I saw the ritual collar flare when K'liira touched the censer. Do you know what the collar responds to, Sister?" She paused, letting the silence build. "It responds to divine resonance. To the goddess's frequency. It does not flare for nothing."

K'liira's breath caught. She had forgotten about the collar. She had been so focused on the smoke, on the spider-shapes curling from the embers, that she hadn't registered the heat building against her throat. Now she felt it again, pulsing in time with her heartbeat, a dark red warmth that felt like accusation.

"The collar flares for every novice during the rites," K'liira said. "It is designed to."

"Is it?" Vesryn tilted her head. "Then explain why mine flared at full intensity when I lit the censer, and yours flared only once — weakly, barely a pulse — when you touched it. Explain why the runes glowed like dying embers while mine burned bright as fresh blood."

More murmurs. K'liira could feel the doubt spreading through the assembly like oil on water. These were girls who had grown up on stories of divine favor, of vision-bearing priestesses who spoke directly to Lolth herself. They had entered the temple hoping to be chosen. And now one of their own was being accused of faking it.

"I have no explanation," K'liira said. "The goddess works in mysterious ways."

Vesryn laughed. It was a short, sharp sound that echoed off the obsidian walls. "Mysterious. Yes. That is the word, isn't it? The word we use when we cannot explain what we do not want to believe." She took one step forward, breaking the spacing protocol, and a dozen heads turned toward her in shock. "I believe the goddess does not speak to K'liira because K'liira does not listen. Or perhaps she listens to something else entirely."

The implication landed like a hammer blow. K'liira felt the blood drain from her face. To suggest that a novice was hearing something other than Lolth — in the temple, in front of the assembled order, in front of Matron Malice — was not merely an accusation of incompetence. It was an accusation of heresy.

Matron Malice raised one hand. The movement was small, almost imperceptible, but the sanctum fell silent instantly. Forty novices held their breath. Even Vesryn stopped talking.

Matron Malice leaned forward slightly. "Sister K'liira. You will explain yourself. After the rites."

The words were simple. The threat behind them was not. K'liira nodded once, her throat too tight to speak.

Vesryn returned to her place without another word, but not before her grey eyes met K'liira's one last time. There was no triumph in them. Only certainty. She believed she had won something today. K'liira wasn't sure whether to pity her or fear her.

The rites resumed. The chanting started again. K'liira opened her mouth and joined the syllables, but the words tasted like ash in her mouth. She could feel the collar burning against her skin, could feel the eyes of forty novices flicking toward her between verses, could feel Matron Malice watching from the dais with the patient attention of a spider waiting for its web to vibrate.

Three months of silence. Three months of listening to the temple breathe in ticks and wondering if the goddess had abandoned them all. And now Vesryn had taken that silence and turned it into a weapon.

K'liira closed her eyes for the briefest moment and reached inward, searching for the hollow space behind her ribs. It was still there. Empty. Aching. But within the ache, something else stirred — not a voice, not a vision, but a memory. The girl's hand in hers. The whisper in the dark. *Thank you.*

She opened her eyes. The chanting continued. Vesryn stood at her side, still and composed, her white-blonde hair gleaming in the fungi-light. And Matron Malice watched, and waited, and smiled the smallest fraction of a smile that made K'liira's blood run cold.

The web had been set. The question was whether K'liira was the fly or the spider.

When the rites finally ended, the novices rose as one and filed out in orderly ranks. K'liira moved with them, keeping her head down, her collar still warm against her throat. She felt Vesryn's presence at her side — not touching, not speaking, just there, like a shadow cast by something brighter.

"You should be careful, Sister," Vesryn murmured, her voice low enough that only K'liira could hear it. "The goddess favors those who deserve her favor. And she abandons those who do not."

K'liira didn't respond. She kept walking. But she felt the words settle into her bones like cold iron, heavy and permanent.

At the edge of the sanctum, where the obsidian floor gave way to the rough-hewn stone of the lower passages, Vesryn stopped. She turned to face K'liira fully, and for a moment, the mockery dropped from her features, replaced by something harder, more calculating.

"They're going to ask questions," Vesryn said. "About the collar. About the prayers. About why a nineteen-cycle-old novice who has never seen the goddess in three years suddenly starts claiming divine contact." She tilted her head. "You need to be ready to answer them. Or you'll find yourself answering them to Matron Malice alone."

Then she turned and walked away, her steps silent on the stone, her white-blonde hair disappearing into the shadows of the passage.

K'liira stood alone in the corridor, listening to the temple breathe in ticks, feeling the collar pulse against her throat like a second heartbeat. Somewhere below her, in the slave levels, she thought she heard the faintest sound — not a tick, not a chant, but a whisper. Faint. Desperate. Asking for help.

She closed her eyes and listened.

The whisper faded. The temple breathed in ticks. And K'liira walked toward the Mirror Chamber, toward the silence, toward whatever Matron Malice had prepared for her.

Behind her, the great sanctum emptied. Before her, the lower passages stretched dark and endless. And somewhere in the space between, the goddess remained silent.

But K'liira was beginning to understand that silence could mean many things. And that the absence of a voice was not the same as the absence of a presence.

She touched the collar at her throat. The runes were cooling now, the red pulse fading to a dull warmth. Almost normal. Almost invisible.

Almost.

K'liira walked on. The ticks followed her down into the dark.


# Chapter 3

The fissure exhaled a draft against her ankles. K'liira knelt at its mouth, her palm flat against the stone beside the hanging lichen curtain, seeking the warmth she expected to find. There was none. The rock beyond was the chill of deep water, a cold that bit through her tunic and settled into her bones. She had spent three hours crawling through passages that smelled of wet fungus and old blood, guided only by the memory of the shadow's hand pointing and the single word that had echoed in her mind: *hide*.

She pushed aside the lichen. It parted like a curtain of wet hair, releasing the scent of something green and decaying, the smell of things that grew in darkness and didn't care about it. The fissure opened wider than it had looked from the outside—wide enough for a body, narrow enough that she had to turn sideways to slip through. Her shoulders scraped the stone on both sides, grinding grit into the skin of her arms. She kept moving.

Behind her, the lower tunnels exhaled. The fungi-lamps still pulsed violet in the distance, their light swallowed by the narrowing passage. Ahead, there was only the cold and the sound of her own breathing, shallow and careful, the way you breathed when you didn't want anything else to hear you.

The fissure ended.

Not with a wall. Not with a dead end. With a room.

K'liira froze in the threshold, one foot inside, one outside, her body caught between the passage she knew and whatever waited in the dark. The room was small—no larger than a slave quarter—but its walls were unlike anything she had seen in the temple or the lower levels. They were carved, not with the jagged spider motifs that covered every surface in House Do'Urden's domain, but with smooth, flowing lines that curved and crossed in patterns that made her eyes ache if she stared too long. The carvings depicted figures—not spiders, not warriors, not the stern faces of matrons and priestesses—but people. Drow, yes, but not as she knew them. Their hands were open. Their heads were tilted upward, not downward toward the floor in submission.

She stepped fully inside and let the lichen fall back behind her.

The air in the room was different. Not colder, exactly, but cleaner. The stale iron-and-blood smell of the temple was absent, replaced by something faint and sweet, like dried flowers pressed between pages of a book no one had opened in centuries. She reached for the censer at her belt out of habit, then stopped herself. There was no flame to carry here. No smoke to read. Just the dim, greenish glow of fungi growing in patches along the ceiling, casting the room in a light that made everything look underwater.

At the center of the room stood an altar.

It was not made of obsidian or basalt or any of the dark stones that dominated the temple's architecture. This was pale stone—white, almost, though the fungi-light made it hard to tell. It could have been marble. It could have been something else entirely. K'liira had never seen marble in Menzoberranzan. She had heard elders speak of it in stories, the kind of stone that caught light and held it, that shimmered instead of absorbing. The altar was rough-hewn, unpolished, its surface scarred by time and touch. On top of it sat a single object: a bowl, shallow and wide, filled with water that was perfectly still and perfectly clear.

Clear water. Underground. Without a source she could see.

K'liira approached slowly, her bare feet making no sound on the stone floor. She knelt beside the altar and peered into the bowl. Her reflection stared back at her—distorted by the curve of the vessel, her dark hair a spill of ink around her face, her eyes wide and violet in the fungal glow. She looked younger in the water. Younger than nineteen. Younger than the girl who had knelt in the House Do’Urden Temple and heard nothing when the censer touched her collar.

Something moved beneath the surface.

She jerked back, her hand flying to the knife at her belt. But there was nothing to cut. The water was still again. Just her reflection. Just the dark shape of the room above her in the bowl's curved surface.

Then she noticed the second object on the altar. A small figurine, carved from the same pale stone. It depicted a woman—not a spider, not a warrior queen, but a woman standing with her arms outstretched, her head tilted toward the ceiling as if listening. As if waiting.

K'liira had never seen a statue of a drow goddess that looked like this. All the images of Lolth in the temple were sharp angles and many limbs, spider legs branching from human torsos, fangs where teeth should be. Even the smaller devotional statues—the ones the novices touched before prayers, the ones the acolytes carried in processions—were angular and menacing. This was soft. Human. The figurine's face was featureless, smoothed by centuries of thumb-rubbing, but the posture was unmistakable. Open hands. Open posture. Receiving.

She reached out and touched the figurine.

The moment her fingers brushed the spore-cluster, the fungi-light above flickered, then deepened into a steady, amber hue that cast long, dancing shadows against the obsidian walls, mimicking the familiar warmth of a hearth rather than the cold, sterile glow of the cavern.

K'liira pulled her hand back as if burned.

The light faded. The fungi returned to their steady green pulse. The bowl of water was still. The figurine sat where it had been, unchanged.

But her fingertips were warm.

Not the residual heat of touched stone. Not the phantom warmth of a censer's coals. Real warmth. The kind of warmth that came from something alive, or something that remembered what alive felt like.

She sat back on her heels and looked around the room more carefully. There were markings on the walls that she had missed at first glance—small, worn, nearly invisible against the carved patterns. Prayers. She recognized the shape of them, though she could not read the words. The script was older than the temple's inscriptions, older than the runes on her ritual collar. The characters were softer, rounder, less aggressive. They did not slash at the stone. They flowed into it.

*Eilistraee.*

The name came to her unbidden, the way a word surfaces from deep water when you've stopped trying to find it. She had heard it once, three years ago, in the slave quarters. A dying boy had whispered it as the grey rot took his lungs, and she had not known what it meant. She had thought it was a name for death. Now, kneeling in this room with its pale altar and its bowl of impossible water, she wondered if it had been a name for something else entirely.

A goddess who listened.

Not a goddess who demanded. Not a goddess who took. A goddess who received.

The realization hit her with the force of a physical blow. She pressed her palms against the stone floor to steady herself, her breath coming fast and shallow. If this was true—if this shrine existed, if someone had kept it here, in the deepest passages beneath the temple, hidden behind a fissure and a curtain of lichen—then everything she had been taught about the goddess was incomplete. Or wrong. Or both.

Lolth was silent. That was the accepted explanation. The Spider Queen had withdrawn her voice as a sign of favor, or perhaps as punishment for the House's failings. Matron Malice had declared it so from the dais, her voice cold and certain, and the other priestesses had nodded and bowed and repeated the explanation until it became doctrine. But what if the silence wasn't about Lolth at all? What if it was about her? What if the goddess hadn't withdrawn her voice—what if she had simply been starved?

K'liira's hands trembled. She pressed them flat against the floor until the shaking stopped.

She had come here to hide, as the shadow had told her to. She had crawled through the fissure because the shadow had pointed and the word *wait* had lodged in her skull like a splinter. But hiding was not what this room wanted. The shrine was not a hiding place. It was an altar. And altars were meant to be used.

She emptied the remaining herbs from her pouch—crushed myrrh and a pinch of salt, leftovers from the preparation table—and poured them into the bowl. The myrrh floated on the surface, tiny golden flakes spreading outward. The salt dissolved instantly, disappearing into the water as if it had never existed. The bowl accepted them without resistance. Without judgment.

K'liira closed her eyes and said nothing. She had taken a vow of silence for three months, a penance imposed by Matron Malice after Vesryn's accusation. Speaking would break it. Speaking would bring consequences she could not afford.

But she didn't need to speak.

She placed her hands on the surface of the water, palm down, fingers spread. The water was cool but not cold. It yielded beneath her touch like a living thing settling into a resting position. She waited.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. This was not the Great Sanctum. There were no flaring runes, no pulses of dark red heat in her collar, no voices in her head. This was a room in the dark with a bowl of water and a carved figurine. She was nineteen years old, kneeling on stone, pretending that faith worked the way stories said it worked.

She opened her eyes.

The figurine had changed.

Not physically—it sat exactly where it had been, on the altar, carved from the same pale stone. But the featureless face was no longer featureless. It had eyes now—two small indentations, carved by time and thumb-rubbing, that she had missed before. And below them, a mouth. Not a smile. Not a frown. An expression she could not name, but one that felt familiar. Like her mother’s slackened visage before the fever took her. Like the slave boy’s grey, bloated face before the rot closed his throat. Waiting. Hoping. Not demanding.

K'liira pulled her hands from the water and stood. Her legs were stiff. Her knees ached. The cold from the fissure seeped up through the stone into her thighs. She should leave. She should crawl back through the passage and return to the dormitory before dawn. Vesryn would notice her absence. Matron Malice would demand an explanation. The collar at her throat would flare if she was caught lying.

But she could not leave empty-handed.

She took the figurine. It was small enough to fit in her palm, light as a bird's bone. She wrapped it in the hem of her robe and tucked it beneath her tunic, pressing it against her skin where she could feel its weight through fabric and flesh. It was warm. Still warm.

As she turned toward the fissure, she noticed something on the wall beside the altar. A marking she had not seen before—or perhaps one she had seen and dismissed. It was a simple symbol: a spiral, drawn in the same soft script as the prayers. Inside the spiral was a single word, carved deep enough that her fingertip caught on the grooves.

She traced it carefully.

*Saya.*

The meaning eluded her, but a certainty settled in her chest like a stone dropped into still water, promising she would find it out.

The fissure was narrower on the way back. Or perhaps she was carrying more weight than she had realized. The figurine pressed against her ribs with every movement, a constant, quiet reminder that she was no longer kneeling alone in the dark.

Behind her, in the room with the pale altar and the bowl of clear water, the fungi continued their slow green pulse. The shrine waited. It had waited for centuries. It could wait a little longer.

K'liira crawled through the fissure and emerged into the lower tunnels. The violet light of the fungi-lamps greeted her like an old friend she had not realized she missed. The air smelled of fungus and wet stone and the faint, metallic tang of old blood. The temple breathed in ticks.

She stood in the passage and listened to them. For the first time, she heard something underneath the ticking. A sound so faint she might have imagined it. A hum. Low and steady, like a voice singing without words.

She turned and walked back toward the dormitory, her steps quieter than they had been on the way down, her body lighter than it should have been, the figurine warm against her skin.

The silence of the goddess had not broken. But something else had.

And K'liira was beginning to understand that silence could be many things. It could be abandonment. It could be punishment. It could be starvation.

Or it could be listening.

She did not yet know which truth held weight, but a certainty frightened her as she waited for the answer to reveal itself.

She reached the dormitory entrance and paused at the threshold. The bodies of the other novices lay in rows on the stone floor, their breathing synchronized, their faces smooth in sleep. Vesryn was there, curled on her side, her white-blonde hair fanned across the stone like spilled milk. She looked peaceful in sleep. K'liira had never seen her look peaceful awake.

K'liira slipped past her without waking her, found her own sleeping mat in the far corner, and lay down on her back, staring at the ceiling. The figurine pressed against her ribs through layers of fabric. She could feel it through the stone, through the rock, through the miles of tunnel and chamber that separated her from the surface world she had never seen.

She closed her eyes.

And for the first time in three months, she dreamed of light that was not violet.

She dreamed of a woman with open hands and a face she could almost recognize. She dreamed of water that was clear and cold and perfectly still. She dreamed of a name—*Saya*—spoken softly, like a mother whispering to a sleeping child in the dark, or a friend murmuring a secret when she believed herself alone.

She woke before dawn. The dormitory was quiet except for the breathing of the other novices and the distant ticking of the temple above. Her hands were still warm. Her ribs still bore the weight of the figurine. And in her chest, where the silence had lived for three months, something new had taken root.

Small. Fragile. Terrifying.

Hope.

She sat up slowly, carefully, so as not to wake anyone. She reached beneath her tunic and touched the figurine through the fabric. It was warm. It had always been warm.

K’liira had no idea what she would do with this knowledge, nor how she might keep it secret. She wondered if the goddess Eilistraee—if that was truly who this shrine belonged to—could help her or hurt her or both.

She knew only that she had found something in the dark that the temple had tried to bury. And that finding it had changed her.

Not dramatically. Not with a voice from the heavens or a flash of divine light. But subtly, seeping into the earth like roots cracking bedrock, wearing down stone through relentless pressure, silencing the air until a single whisper shattered the quiet.

She stood and dressed in the dark, pulling her robe tight around her shoulders, securing the figurine beneath her tunic where no one would see it. She would return to the rituals. She would kneel in the Great Sanctum and bow her head and touch the censer to her collar and pretend that the silence meant nothing had changed.

But it had changed.

Everything had changed.

And when Vesryn looked at her that morning with her sharp grey eyes and her mocking smile, K'liira would meet her gaze without flinching, because she would carry a secret that Vesryn could not touch, could not sabotage, could not take.

A secret carved in pale stone and filled with clear water and humming with the memory of a name.

*Saya.*

She stepped out of the dormitory and into the passage, the ticks of the temple surrounding her like a heartbeat, the violet fungi-lamps casting their familiar glow over the stone walls, the air thick with the smell of iron and ozone and old blood.

She walked toward the Great Sanctum.

She walked with her hands folded over the place where hope lived.

The morning rites began. The censer was brought in. The smoke curled upward, twisting into shapes that looked like spiders and then dissolved into nothing. The ritual collar at K'liira's throat flared faintly—a pulse of dark red heat that faded quickly, barely a ripple. Matron Malice's eyes passed over her, cold and assessing, and moved on.

K'liira knelt and listened and said nothing, as she had been ordered to do. But inside, beneath the silence she maintained with her lips and her voice, something new stirred. Something warm and fragile and terrifying.

Something that sounded, if she listened closely enough, like a name.

*Saya.*

The rites passed. The novices rose. Vesryn stood and turned, her grey eyes locking onto K'liira's with an intensity that made the air between them feel charged. She said nothing. She didn't need to. Her expression said everything: *I know something is different about you. I can feel it. Tell me what you've found.*

K'liira said nothing. She simply looked back, her violet eyes steady, her face calm, her hands folded in her lap where no one could see the tremor that threatened to take root.

*Nothing,* her expression said. *I haven't found anything.*

Vesryn studied her for a long moment. Then she turned and walked away, her steps quick and precise, her white-blonde hair swinging behind her like a banner.

K'liira exhaled. She had bought herself time. Not much. But enough.

She stood and joined the other novices as they filed out of the Great Sanctum, her steps measured, her body controlled, her mind already turning over the next steps she needed to take. She needed to learn more about Eilistraee. She needed to find out who had maintained the shrine. She needed to decide whether this was faith or obsession, revelation or madness.

She needed to be careful.

In Menzoberranzan, the wrong kind of revelation got you killed. The right kind got you killed too, if you weren't prepared for the cost.

She walked through the passage, the ticks of the temple surrounding her, the violet fungi-lamps casting their familiar glow over the stone walls, the figurine warm against her ribs.

She walked with her hands folded over the place where hope lived.

And she walked knowing that she was no longer the same girl who had knelt in the Great Sanctum three months ago and heard nothing.

She was someone new.

Someone who had found a name in the dark.

Someone who was willing to listen.

Someone who was willing to risk everything for a goddess who had been forgotten.

The passage stretched ahead of her, endless and dark and full of possibility.

She walked into it.

Without looking back.

The temple breathed in ticks. The silence held. And somewhere beneath it all, in a room no one had visited in centuries, a goddess who had been forgotten began to remember what it felt like to be heard.

K'liira did not know this. She only knew the warmth in her tunic and the certainty in her chest and the hum that might have been nothing at all.

She walked through the dark.

And for the first time in three months, she was not afraid of the silence.

She was afraid of what it might say.

When it finally chose to speak.

She would be ready.

Whatever the cost.

Whatever the price.

She would be ready.

Vesryn studied her face for a long moment. Her expression shifted—calculating, reassessing, adjusting. She was not convinced. But she was not certain either. And uncertainty was useful. Uncertainty made people careless. Vesryn smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had decided to wait.

"We'll see," she said.

Then she turned and walked away, her steps quick and light, disappearing into the intersection ahead like a thread pulled through fabric.

K'liira stood alone in the corridor. The fungi-lamps pulsed their steady violet rhythm above her. The temple breathed in ticks. And beneath everything, the figurine pressed warm against her ribs, a secret she could not share, could not prove, could not abandon.

She walked on.

She moved through the temple's corridors the way a hunter moved through the wilds—knowing every turn, every shortcut, every blind corner where no one would see her coming. She passed three other acolytes on her way, nodding to them with the casual familiarity of someone who belonged everywhere, who was owed attention in every room. House Do'Urden's elite did not sneak. For matters that did not require the full weight of the House's presence.

Her face was sharp and severe, her black hair pulled back so tightly that the skin at her temples looked drawn. Her eyes were black—not the dark brown that most drow possessed, but true black, the color of a pupil in complete darkness, the color of the space between stars.

Vesryn entered and bowed deeply, her forehead touching the obsidian floor. She held the position for three heartbeats—the proper amount of time for someone of her station addressing a matron. When she rose, she kept her gaze lowered. Respect was a performance, and Vesryn was excellent at performances.

Vesryn hesitated for half a heartbeat—the calculated hesitation of someone who was choosing their words carefully, not the genuine hesitation of someone who was unsure. "I observed her this morning. Her eyes are shadowed. Her hands tremble, though she conceals it. She carries herself differently than she did three days ago. Lighter, in a way that suggests she has discovered something. # Chapter 5

The Second Descent began at dusk, when the fungi-lamps flared their deepest violet and the temple's ticks seemed to slow, as if the building itself was holding its breath. K'liira stood at the top of the stairwell, her bare feet pressed against the warm obsidian, feeling the residual heat from the day's rites radiating upward through the stone. Horror.

Not the theatrical horror of novice dramas, the kind performed in the dormitory at night with whispers and gasps and hands pressed over mouths. Real horror. The image was the knife.

K'liira's hand moved before her mind could stop it.

She grabbed Matron Malice's wrist.

The cavern went silent. Silence.

Someone pressed her face against the obsidian, and she could taste the metal in it—the iron residue of centuries of blood sacrifices, the copper tang of old violence, the salt of tears that had fallen on this same stone and evaporated and been absorbed and forgotten.

And beneath everything, the hum was gone.

Completely.

She touched the figurine at her ribs. It was cool now. No longer warm. No longer humming. Just stone. Just a thing. Just a memory.

K'liira closed her eyes. And listened to the silence.
