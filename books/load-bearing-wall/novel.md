# The Load-Bearing Wall

# Chapter 1

The fluorescent lights hummed at a frequency Steve had learned to ignore, the same way he'd learned to ignore the pile of unpaid vendor invoices sitting on his desk, the same way he ignored the fact that it was barely Tuesday and he'd already checked his email fourteen times. He stood in aisle seven of the Flagship Store, clipboard pressed against his ribs like a shield, and counted boxes of drywall anchors.

One. Two. Three. Four.

He liked counting. Counting was honest. A number either matched or it didn't. There was no ambiguity in arithmetic, unlike the silent treatment from his son, who hadn't spoken to him directly in three weeks except to demand the charger or complain about the Wi-Fi, or the look Marla gave him—sharp, assessing, as if he were a spreadsheet she couldn't reconcile.

Doug came around the corner pushing a cart loaded with return merchandise, his uniform shirt hanging loose on a frame that had shrunk ten years but refused to accept it. He smelled like Old Spice and peppermint, the cologne-and-candy combination that had been his signature since the nineties.

"You're doing the deep count again," Doug said. Not a question. He'd been saying it for six months.

"It's Q2 inventory prep. Corporate wants accuracy to the decimal."

"The decimal." Doug shook his head. "Steve, it's screws. It's not the Rosetta Stone."

"These are toggle bolts. Different load-bearing ratings depending on drywall thickness. If we ship the wrong spec to a contractor—"

"—they'll call us back and we'll send the right ones. We've been doing this forty-two years, kid. I think we can handle a wrong shipment of metal pieces."

Steve marked his clipboard. He wanted to say something sharp. He wanted to say *you don't understand supply chain optimization* or *if you'd just use the new scanner app instead of walking around pretending the barcodes are a conspiracy*. But Doug was sixty-two and had been here since the store opened in 1981, and Steve's mother had hired him personally because she said he had "good hands." So Steve said nothing and counted.

Five, six, seven, eight.

The ceiling above them was a grid of acoustic tiles, water-stained in places, some missing entirely to reveal the metal t-bar framework beneath. Steve had filed three work orders for roof repair in the last six months. Each one had been forwarded to regional, then to corporate facilities management, then to a queue labeled PRIORITY_LOW that Steve suspected no one ever opened. He knew this because he'd logged into the portal himself last Thursday and seen the queue. Twelve items ahead of the roof. All of them fluorescent light ballasts in stores that sold less annual revenue than the cost of replacing the ballasts.

"Heavy rain yesterday," Doug said, leaning against a display of extension cords. "Saw some pooling near the east loading dock."

I'll have Mei-Lin Chen check the gutters. She's been meaning to—

Steve." Doug's voice dropped half an octave, a register reserved for confessions and bad news. "That roof hasn't been touched since '78. The shingles are older than you are."

"I know the building's age, Doug."

"No, you don't. You know the warranty dates. You know the depreciation schedule. You know the square footage and the foot traffic metrics and the conversion rates from window shoppers to buyers. You don't know that the trusses are cedar and that the original contractor put in extra bracing because the foreman's kid used to ride his bike through the parking lot and he didn't want a sheet of tin to fall on her head."

Steve stared at him. Doug was looking up at the ceiling now, his face tilted toward the water stains as if searching for a draft of warm air.

"How do you know that?" Steve asked.

"My dad was the foreman." Doug tapped his temple. "I remember her. She grew up. Had kids. I saw her at the grocery store last year. He was a good man. He built things to last."

The hum of the fluorescents seemed louder. Steve looked up at the tiles, really looked at them, and noticed for the first time how many were sagging. Not dramatically. Not in any way that would show up on a safety inspection report. Just enough—half an inch, maybe—that if you pressed on them they'd give. Like teeth with decay underneath enamel that looked fine.

A thin line of dust trickled from the seam where two beams met, coating the linoleum in a fine gray snow. One of the metal t-bars groaned under the weight of wet insulation, bending imperceptibly but permanently, the way a knee bends when the ligament is already torn and you're just waiting to find out.

"Maybe we should get the roof looked at," Steve said.

"We should have gotten it looked at in 2003. And 2011. And 2016." Doug pushed off the extension cord display and started rolling his cart down the aisle. "Come on. Let's finish this section before lunch. I'm thinking chili dogs."

He ran the numbers in his head again. If they closed the back warehouse, they could afford the roof. They could keep the front alive, even if the body had to shrink. He pushed the thought away. Closing the warehouse meant laying off three people. It meant admitting defeat before the fight had even begun.

Steve followed. He told himself it was because Doug was right about finishing the count. He told himself it was because skipping a section would throw off the totals and he'd have to come back and double-count anyway. But the truth, which he acknowledged only in the private chamber of his mind where he kept things he wouldn't say out loud, was that Doug talking about his father made Steve feel something he couldn't name, and staying close to Doug felt like staying close to something solid in a world that had become increasingly difficult to stand on.

They reached aisle nine. The store was quiet except for the distant clatter of Mei-Lin reorganizing the paint department somewhere to their left—Steve could hear her humming, a tuneless sound that indicated concentration. The afternoon sun filtered through the high clerestory windows, casting long rectangles of gold across the linoleum. Everything was ordinary. Everything was exactly what a Tuesday should be.

Then the roof groaned.

It wasn't loud at first. A low, resonant sound, like a cello string bowed too hard, vibrating through the floorboards and up through the soles of Steve's shoes. He stopped. Doug stopped. They looked at each other.

"Do you hear that?" Steve said.

"Hear what?"

The groaning grew louder. It wasn't one sound anymore—it was many, layered on top of each other, the sound of metal bending and wood splitting and nails pulling free from joints that had held for decades. The acoustic tiles above them began to tremble as slow motes of dust drifted down, catching the sunlight like gold leaf.

"Move," Doug said. His voice was flat and certain, the way a man's voice becomes when he stops thinking and starts acting.

They ran.

Steve ran toward the nearest exit, his clipboard flying from his hands, his shoes slipping on the suddenly slick linoleum—water, he realized, water dripping from above. Doug ran the opposite direction, toward the back, toward the paint department where Mei-Lin was working. Steve shouted her name but the sound was swallowed by the noise, which was now a roar, the sound of a building tearing itself apart from the top down.

He turned and ran back. Not away—from. Toward Doug. It wasn't a decision so much as a reflex, the same way you reach for a railing when you stumble, the same way you grab a child without thinking. Doug was under the eastern span, near the lighting fixtures, and the ceiling was coming down in sections, great sheets of tile and insulation and something darker and heavier that Steve didn't want to identify.

"Doug!"

His father's voice answered, distant and calm, and then Steve understood that Doug wasn't in danger. He was moving with purpose, guiding Mei-Lin and two stock associates toward the emergency exit, his arm around Mei-Lin's shoulders, his voice cutting through the chaos like a blade.

Steve turned toward the front. The main entrance was visible through the haze of falling dust, bright with afternoon light. He could make it. He could be out.

Something caught his eye—a shape in the debris, a hand reaching up from beneath a collapsed section of ceiling, fingers splayed, palm facing outward in a gesture that might have been a wave or might have been a plea.

Doug.

Steve ran toward it.

The world narrowed to the space between him and Doug, to the dust filling his lungs and the heat of the collapsing structure pressing down from above like a hand on his shoulders. He dove. He grabbed Doug's wrist. He pulled.

Doug pulled back. He shook his head. He pointed—not away, but deeper into the store, toward the hardware section, where the ceiling had caved in completely and the space beyond was dark and impossibly vast.

Steve didn't understand. Then he did. Doug was telling him to go. To get help. To find someone who could pull him out properly.

"No," Steve said. He dug his heels into the linoleum. "No. I'm not leaving you."

Doug's face was gray with dust. His eyes were bright. He said something Steve couldn't hear over the groaning, something that might have been *go* or might have been *I love you* or might have been both. He squeezed Steve's hand once, hard, and then let go.

Steve stood there for one second. Two. Three. The ceiling above Doug gave another terrible crack, and a beam shifted, and Steve knew—if he stayed, he would die, and if he went, Doug might live.

He ran.

He ran past the paint department where Mei-Lin stood frozen, her arms full of color swatches, her mouth open in a silent scream. He ran past the checkout lanes where customers were scattered like pins, some crying, some filming, all of them moving in directions that had nothing to do with each other. He ran out the front doors and into the blinding afternoon sun, his lungs burning, his ears ringing, his hands shaking so badly he couldn't zip up his jacket.

The parking lot stretched before him, vast and ordinary, cars parked in neat rows under a sky that had no idea anything had happened. Steve fell to his knees. He put his forehead against the warm asphalt and breathed. He breathed in the smell of hot tar and gasoline and cut grass, and he thought of Doug's hand in his, and he thought of the roof, and he thought of the twelve work orders in the PRIORITY_LOW queue, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like violence, that he had been counting screws while the building fell apart above his head.

Sirens wailed in the distance. Somewhere behind him, the store continued to settle, its bones shifting and settling into a new shape, a shape that would never be what it had been. Steve closed his eyes. He waited for the paramedics. He waited for the police. He waited for whatever came next, which was nothing he had planned for and everything he needed.

The fluorescent lights inside the store went out one by one, clicking into darkness like a row of teeth being pulled, until only emergency exit signs remained, their green glow painting the chaos in an eerie, underwater light.

And Doug, pinned beneath the eastern span with his elbow broken and his ribs bruised and his left boot crushed, laughed. He laughed because the pain was sharp and immediate and therefore manageable, because Steve was alive, and because for the first time in forty-two years he was going to have to ask someone else to count the screws.

Mei-Lin stood in the parking lot clutching her color swatches, watching the smoke curl from the roof like a signal fire, and thought about how she would organize the aftermath, imposing the same rigid grid systems she used to manage the paint department on this chaos, because if she didn't organize it, she would have to feel it, and feeling it would break her.

Steve sat on the curb with his head in his hands and tried to remember the exact number of toggle bolts in aisle seven. He got to eleven before he stopped. Eleven out of an unknown total. An incomplete count. A number that meant nothing without the rest of the data. He thought about this for a long time, while the sirens grew louder and the world rearranged itself around him, and he understood that some things cannot be counted. Some things can only be survived.

The afternoon light shifted. The shadows lengthened. The store, which had stood since 1978, which had housed millions of transactions and countless small human dramas—arguments over prices, discoveries of forgotten tools, the quiet satisfaction of fixing something that was broken—settled into its new shape. Steve watched it happen from the outside, through the shattered front windows, and he felt something he hadn't felt in years: the sensation of standing still while everything around him moved, of being powerless in the face of forces larger than himself, of having to trust that the people inside were stronger than he was.

He was wrong about that, eventually. But not yet. Not in this moment. In this moment, he was just a man on a curb, counting nothing at all, watching his life collapse in real time and realizing that counting had never been the point.

The point was the people. The point was the hands. The point was the cedar trusses and the extra bracing and the foreman's daughter who grew up and had kids and rode her bike through the parking lot and lived.

Steve opened his eyes. The paramedics were approaching. He stood up. His legs shook. He brushed the asphalt from his pants and walked toward them, his footsteps uneven, his breath ragged, his mind empty of numbers for the first time in twenty years.

Behind him, the store exhaled—a final, settling groan, like a beast curling into sleep—and then silence.

The silence was worse.


# Chapter 2

The parking lot smelled like wet concrete and whatever chemical the paramedics had sprayed near Steve's boots. He sat in the driver's seat of his pickup truck with the engine off, hands gripping the steering wheel at ten and two the way he'd been taught since he was sixteen and his dad had sat in the passenger seat correcting his grip. His knuckles were white. He didn't notice.

Behind him, the store stood like something that had been punched in the face and was trying very hard to pretend it wasn't hurt. The north wall was intact. The roof over the electronics section had pancaked into a jagged fan of corrugated metal and drywall dust that caught the afternoon light like ground glass. Steve watched it. He watched it for a long time. He tried to count the visible damage the way he counted boxes of drywall anchors—methodically, systematically, each item catalogued and filed away—but the numbers wouldn't come. There were no numbers here. Just the shape of what he'd broken.

His phone buzzed on the passenger seat. He didn't touch it. It buzzed again. Then again. Fourteen times today already. He'd checked his email fourteen times before the roof fell. He'd checked it while standing in aisle seven holding a clipboard he wasn't using. He'd checked it while waiting for Doug to finish his inventory count in aisle nine. He'd checked it while the ceiling above him began to sing that low groaning note that meant something was giving way somewhere, and he hadn't looked up because he was reading a vendor notification about delayed shipments of shelving brackets.

The phone stopped buzzing. The silence was worse than the buzzing.

Steve leaned his forehead against the steering wheel. The plastic was warm from the sun. He pressed harder. He wanted to feel something other than the hollow ache behind his ribs, the one that had been growing for years and had finally cracked something open when the roof came down. He kept pressing until his nose was flat against the plastic and his breath fogged the windshield in small, irregular clouds.

He started crying.

It wasn't dramatic. There was no sobbing, no loud heaving of the shoulders. It was the kind of crying that happens when a man has held his breath for too long and finally lets it out and discovers he's been holding more than breath. Tears tracked through the dust on his cheeks—drywall dust, probably, from the collapse—and his mouth opened slightly on each exhale like he was trying to breathe through a wound. He kept his hands on the wheel. He kept his eyes closed. He sat in the truck and cried in the parking lot of a store that used to belong to him and now belonged to insurance adjusters and structural engineers and people whose names he didn't know but who would decide whether three generations of his family's livelihood survived the afternoon.

Behind him, a car door closed.

Steve didn't turn around. He heard the footsteps—heavy, uneven, familiar. Doug. Doug moved like a man who had spent forty-three years walking through a hardware store, navigating aisles stacked shoulder-high with things he could identify by sound alone. The footsteps stopped at the driver's window. Steve felt Doug's shadow fall across the passenger seat.

"Steve?"

Steve wiped his face with the back of his hand. It came away gray. He tried to smile. It didn't work.

"I'm fine," he said. His voice sounded wrong—cracked, too high, like someone else's voice that had been borrowed and returned damaged.

Doug didn't say anything for a moment. Then he said, "Boy, you're sitting in your truck in the middle of the parking lot crying like a schoolboy and telling me you're fine."

Steve looked at him through the open window. Doug stood there in his blue work shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbows, white hair catching the late afternoon sun. His hazel eyes were soft in a way Steve rarely saw them. Soft and worried. The way a man looks at something he's known for decades and realizes he doesn't understand it at all.

"I didn't mean to—" Steve started. He stopped. He couldn't finish the sentence. He couldn't say *I didn't mean to break the roof*. He couldn't say *I didn't mean to fail you*. He couldn't say *I didn't mean to fail myself*.

Doug reached into his shirt pocket and pulled out a crumpled pack of cigarettes. He looked at them, looked at Steve, and put them back. He'd quit five years ago. He still carried the pack like a talisman.

"You want me to call your wife?" Doug asked.

"No." Steve shook his head. "Not yet."

"Your boy's here."

Steve turned. He hadn't heard Owen arrive. He shouldn't have been surprised—Owen moved with the ghostly silence of someone who spent twelve hours a day wearing noise-canceling headphones, filtering out a world that was always too loud. Owen stood ten feet away, phone raised, screen pointed directly at Steve's truck.

Steve spun around, startled by a presence he hadn’t sensed. It wasn’t surprising; Owen had mastered the art of moving silently, gliding through the noisy world like someone accustomed to blocking out the world with noise-canceling headphones. Ten feet away, Owen held his phone up, the screen aimed squarely at Steve’s truck, the red recording dot blinking with steady, accusatory rhythm. At Steve.

"What are you doing?" Steve said. His voice was sharp now, defensive, the jargon-laced panic of a man who reaches for control and finds only empty air.

Owen lowered the phone slightly. His expression was unreadable—the flat, neutral mask of a teenager who had perfected the art of appearing uninterested in everything while secretly caring about every single thing.

"Just filming," Owen said.

"For what?"

"Mom's going to see this," Owen added, almost casually, the way a kid mentions he'd already sent a text. He didn't seem to think about it. He just filmed. That was the thing about phones now—they made everything feel temporary, like you could capture a moment and drop it back into the world like a stone skipped across a pond. Except stones sank. Videos didn't. They multiplied.

Steve felt the cold dread of being witnessed by an audience that never sleeps. It wasn't just Owen watching him. It was everyone Owen knew, and everyone they knew, and strangers who would find it weeks later and scroll past without stopping. The idea of it sat in his chest like a stone.

Owen shrugged. "For my TikTok. It's going to be good."

Steve opened the truck door and stepped out. His legs felt like they belonged to someone else—weak, trembling, not quite connected to his body. He walked toward Owen with the slow, deliberate movements of a man who knew that sudden motion might make something worse break.

"Give me the phone," Steve said.

Owen tilted his head. "No."

"Owen—"

"It's just a video, Owen. Nobody's gonna—

"Give me the phone."

Owen's expression shifted. Something flickered across his face—confusion, maybe, or the dawning realization that his father wasn't joking. That this wasn't a game. That the man who had spent fifteen years teaching Owen to never show weakness was currently standing in a parking lot with tears on his face and a voice that sounded like it was breaking.

"Dad, I'm just—"

"Give me the phone, Owen. Now."

Owen hesitated. Steve could see the calculation happening behind those brown eyes—the rapid assessment of risk versus reward, the same calculus Owen applied to every decision in his life. Give up the phone and lose leverage. Keep it and risk losing something bigger. Owen made his choice. He extended the phone, arm straight, screen facing away from Steve like he was handing over a weapon.

Steve took it. His hands were shaking so badly he almost dropped it. He looked at the screen. The video was still recording. A red dot pulsed in the corner. Steve's own face filled the frame—hunched in the truck seat, forehead against the steering wheel, shoulders shaking with silent sobs. The angle was perfect. Or terrible. He couldn't tell which anymore. Perfect angles made good videos. Terrible angles made honest ones. This was both.

Steve turned the phone off. He handed it back to Owen without looking at him.

"Go home," Steve said.

"Dad—"

"Go home, Owen. Tell your mother I'll call her later."

Owen didn't move. He stood there, phone in hand, watching his father with an expression Steve couldn't parse. It wasn't anger. It wasn't disappointment. It was something quieter and more complicated—something that looked like pity, which was the worst possible thing a fifteen-year-old could offer his father.

"You're weird," Owen said finally. Then he turned and walked away, his sneakers scuffing against the wet asphalt, his shoulders hunched against the cold wind that had picked up since noon.

Steve watched him go. He wanted to call him back. He wanted to say sorry. He wanted to say *I'm not okay* and *I don't know what I'm doing* and *please help me figure out how to be a father when I've spent my whole life figuring out how to be a manager*. But the words stayed lodged in his throat, thick and useless, the way they always did when it mattered most.

Doug was still standing by the truck. He hadn't moved. He'd seen everything.

"You alright, boy?" Doug asked again.

Steve shook his head. He couldn't speak. He got back into the truck, closed the door, and sat there with his hands on the wheel again, staring at the place where Owen had disappeared between two rows of compact cars.

The wind carried the smell of rain. Steve closed his eyes. He thought about the drywall anchors he'd been counting. Four. Three. Two. One. The numbers came back slow, reluctant, like guests arriving late to a party they weren't sure they were invited to. He counted them anyway. He always counted them. It was the only thing he knew how to do when the world stopped making sense.

Behind him, the store continued to settle. Small sounds—pings and cracks and the occasional groan—rose from the wreckage like a language Steve was only beginning to understand. He sat in the truck and listened to it, letting the sounds fill the spaces where his composure used to live.

When he finally started the engine, the radio played a commercial for a mattress store. Steve turned it off. He drove home in silence, his hands steady now, his face dry, his mind already moving through the calculations that would determine whether the store survived, whether his family survived, whether he survived any of it at all.

He didn’t know about the video Owen had uploaded. He didn’t know that by midnight it would have three thousand views, or that by morning it would have thirty thousand, and by the end of the week it would have three million, and that the caption would read simply: *my dad crying in his truck lol*.

He didn't want to know any of that, because ignorance was the only mercy left to him. He drove home and parked in the driveway and sat in the truck for another ten minutes, listening to the house breathe through its windows—the refrigerator cycling on, the furnace kicking in, the quiet domestic machinery of a family that had stopped speaking to each other months ago and was only now discovering that silence had become permanent.

Steve opened the door and stepped onto the driveway. The grass was wet. The sky was the color of wet concrete. He walked toward the front door and pushed it open, stepping into the kitchen where the bills were piled on the counter and Marla's manuscript drafts were stacked in a tower beside the sink, and where the coffee pot sat cold and empty, and where everything smelled exactly the way it had been smelling for months—stale and unresolved and waiting for something to happen that neither of them knew how to ask for.

The kitchen smelled like stale coffee and the particular sourness that came from leaving paper on a counter long enough for humidity to warp the edges. Steve stood in the doorway with his left hand on the frame, right hand holding a mug that had gone cold twenty minutes ago, and watched his wife sort through the tower of her own manuscript drafts the way a librarian might sort through returned books—checking spines, noting creases, deciding what belonged on the shelf and what belonged in the bin.

She didn't look up when he entered. She never did anymore, not the way she used to. Before, Marla would turn toward sound—the click of the front door, the rumble of the truck idling in the driveway, the specific cadence of his boots on the linoleum that told her whether he was coming in happy or coming in tired. Now she sorted in silence, and the silence had become its own language, one Steve hadn't bothered to learn.

"The bills are on the left," he said. His voice sounded wrong in the small room—too loud, too flat, the way a man speaks in a church he doesn't believe in. "I called the regional office this morning. They want a damage assessment by Friday."

Marla's fingers paused on a page. She was reading her own words back to herself—sentences she'd written months ago, when the world still operated on a schedule she could predict. Steve caught a fragment: *the architecture of absence*. He looked away. He wasn't supposed to read that. He hadn't been supposed to read any of it for weeks, but sometimes late at night, when the house settled and the refrigerator kicked on and he couldn't sleep, he'd pull one of the drafts from the stack and read a paragraph or two, the way a tourist reads a museum plaque without understanding the exhibit.

"Friday," she said. Not a question. An acknowledgment. She placed the page on top of another page, aligned the corners precisely, and set the growing stack aside. Then she reached into the folder beside her elbow and pulled out a document that was thicker than the bills, stapled in the upper left corner, typed on paper that cost more than the printer paper Steve used for inventory lists.

She set it on the counter between them.

Steve stared at it. The document had a header he recognized—legal letterhead, the kind of paper that implied someone else would read it and care about what was inside. He didn't want to touch it. He wanted to pick up the mug and drink the cold coffee and pretend he'd misheard her and go back to the store and count drywall anchors until the numbers made sense again.

"It's not a threat," she said. Her voice was dry, precise, understated—the same voice she used when she told him the milk was out, or when she reminded him that Owen had a parent-teacher conference he'd forgotten about, or when she said *please don't take the truck* and he took it anyway. "It's a request. For space."

Steve looked at the document. The pages were numbered. There were sections with headings he didn't want to read. He could see the shape of it from here—a life being divided into columns, assets allocated, responsibilities assigned, a marriage reduced to line items the way he reduced inventory to SKUs and reorder points. He'd done it a thousand times in spreadsheets. He'd never imagined it applied to this.

"Space," he repeated. The word felt insufficient. It was a word you used for parking spots and storage units, not for the thing that had been happening between them for months—the slow retreat, the careful avoidance, the way they'd learned to navigate each other like furniture in a room they both occupied but neither owned.

"I can't do this right now, Steve," she said. She finally looked at him. Her green eyes were steady, the way they got when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "Not with the store collapsing. Not with Owen filming you in the parking lot like you're some kind of public service announcement. Not with you checking your email fourteen times a day and pretending it matters."

He flinched. He hated that she knew about the fourteen. He'd told her once, defensively, in a tone that suggested she was interrogating him, and she'd absorbed the number like data and filed it somewhere he couldn't reach.

"I'm trying to fix things," he said.

"You're trying to fix the building," she corrected gently. "There's a difference."

Steve set the mug down on the counter. His hand shook slightly—he'd noticed it in the mirror that morning, the tremor in his right hand, the way his fingers couldn't hold a pen steady when he signed a vendor invoice—and he curled his fingers into a fist to hide it. The kitchen felt smaller than it used to. Maybe it was because Marla had taken up less space in it, physically and otherwise, and the room had adjusted to fill the void with something heavier than square footage.

"What happens if I sign it?" he asked. He didn't want to ask it. The words came out anyway, small and brittle, the way a man speaks when he's standing on ice he doesn't trust.

Marla considered the question. She folded her hands on the counter, palms down, fingers interlaced. Steve noticed a smudge of ink on her left thumb—she'd been writing again, the way she did when she was thinking, the fountain pen she'd bought at a shop in downtown that she never used in front of him because she knew he didn't understand why anyone would use anything but a ballpoint.

"You move into the guest room," she said. "Owen stays here. You figure out what you're going to do with the store and with yourself, and I figure out what I'm going to do with the next forty years. We talk once a week about Owen. We don't talk about anything else unless something is urgent. If it gets to the point where we can't stand each other in the same room—which, frankly, Steve, we're already at—then we see a lawyer and we make it permanent."

She said it all without raising her voice. Without anger. That was the worst part. If she'd been angry, Steve could have met anger with anger. He could have defended himself. He could have talked about the store, about the inventory, about the employees who depended on him, about the three generations of Roberts men who'd built something from nothing and handed it down like a heirloom tool. But Marla wasn't angry. She was resigned. And resignation was something he couldn't argue with, couldn't spreadsheet, couldn't fix with visible labor and calloused hands.

"How long have you been writing this?" he asked, nodding at the document.

"Since November."

November. Three months. He'd been in the store every day. He'd come home at eight, eaten dinner in silence, gone to bed at ten, woken at five, and repeated the cycle. He'd told himself this was what commitment looked like. He'd told himself that if he just held the line, if he just kept the machinery running, the rest would sort itself out. He'd been wrong.

"Can I read it?" he asked.

Marla hesitated. Then she slid the document toward him, just an inch, the way you might offer a live thing to someone who'd forgotten how to hold it.

Steve picked it up. The pages were warm from being handled. He flipped to the first page. The heading read *Proposed Separation Agreement – Draft for Discussion*. Below it, in Marla's neat handwriting—a script he'd admired once, when they'd been young and she'd written him notes on grocery lists that made him laugh out loud in the checkout line—were sections he recognised from a law magazine he'd skimmed years ago, back when magazines arrived in the mail and he read them on the toilet instead of checking email.

*Division of Assets.* *Custody Arrangements.* *Temporary Living Arrangements.*

He stopped reading. He put the document down carefully, aligning the edges with the counter, the way Marla had aligned her manuscript pages. His hands were still shaking. He tucked them into his pockets.

"I need to go to the store," he said.

"I know," Marla said. "Take the afternoon. Owen needs to eat something that isn't energy drink and chips. And Steve?"

He looked up.

"Don't check your email."

He pushed through the automatic doors, the hum of the cooling units fading into the quiet of the evening, leaving the weight of the building’s future suspended in the air between him and the parking lot.


# Chapter 3

The auto parts store on West Central Avenue had fluorescent lights that flickered at a frequency Steve could feel behind his eyes, a low-grade migraine that pulsed in time with the overhead buzz. He stood in the shoe section—which was really just a rack of rubber soles and cardboard displays—and stared at a wall of steel-toed boots the way he used to stare at inventory spreadsheets, searching for patterns that would tell him what to do next.

They were arranged by size, by price, by brand. Carhartt, Timberland, Red Wing. Each pair sitting in its little cardboard coffin, staring back at him with laces tied in perfect bows. Steve reached out and touched the toe cap of a pair in his size. Cold steel. Solid. He pressed harder. His finger left a smudge on the matte finish.

He was forty-seven years old and he had never bought work boots before in his life. His dad had worn them—brown leather, scuffed at the shins, the steel toes dented from decades of dropped wrenches and kicked pallets—but Steve had always worn dress shoes. Brown oxfords. The kind that required a belt buckle the same shade of brown. The kind that said you managed people, not that you worked with them. That was the distinction his father had drawn, anyway, back when Steve was old enough to understand that shoes were a statement.

He held the blueprints with both hands, pressing down on the corners to keep them from fluttering in the draft that moved through the skeletal remains of the building. His hands were still shaking. "Dad."

He laid it on top of the blueprints and tried to align the two documents the way he used to align inventory reports—cross-referencing, checking figures, finding the discrepancies. But the county report was typed in a font he didn't recognize, clean and impersonal, and the blueprints were hand-drawn in someone else's handwriting from another century, and there was no way to make them agree because they weren't speaking the same language. "Does it matter?"

"He said that this morning."

He'd been thinking about the building as a whole, as a system of interlocking parts that needed to be preserved in their current configuration until he could figure out the master plan. Move to the next part.

The drywall panels stretched up to the ceiling joists, eighteen feet or more, and the lower sections were dark with moisture damage—brown stains spreading outward from the base like ink on blotting paper.

"See?" he said. "It's spongy. Water's gotten in behind it."

Steve crouched beside him. He pressed his own hand against the panel. It felt the same—soft, compromised, ready to fail. He looked at Owen’s hand on the wall: fingers splayed wide, wrist bent at a sharp, practiced angle that betrayed experience. Owen’s hands were smaller than Steve’s, cleaner, but they shared the same basic shape—the same broad palm, the same slightly crooked pinky finger that had been bent wrong since a baseball accident when he was eight and Steve had been too busy with a vendor meeting to notice until the swelling went down and the bone had set wrong.

"Okay," he said. "Mark the lines. I'll hold the panel."

Steve held the panel steady, feeling the vibration travel up his arms, feeling the way the drywall responded to the cutting—brittle at first, then giving way in a controlled fracture that Owen directed with practiced precision.

They worked in silence for a while. The only sound was the utility knife and the occasional crack of the drywall separating from the studs. Steve watched Owen’s hands, steady as a surgeon’s, and felt the sharp contrast to his own tremor. Owen’s grip never faltered. Steve had noticed that once, years ago, when Owen had helped him change a tire and his small hands had gripped the lug wrench without hesitation while Steve’s had fumbled with the locking nut. Steve had been proud then. He was proud now, and the pride felt like a stone in his stomach.

After twenty minutes, Owen stepped back and looked at his work. The lower section of drywall was scored all the way around, a rectangular outline that separated cleanly from the rest of the panel. He pressed his palms against the cut section and pushed. The drywall came free with a crack that echoed through the empty space, landing on the concrete with a cloud of dust.

Steve coughed. Owen didn't.

"Again?" Owen asked.

"Again."

They worked for another hour. Panel after panel came down. Owen's rhythm was methodical—score, push, stack. He arranged the removed panels in a neat pile against the far wall, each one flat and aligned, the way Mei-Lin would organize inventory. Steve held the panels, then helped Owen carry them to the stack. His arms ached. His hands bled where the broken edges of the drywall had caught his skin. He didn't notice.

"It's bad," he said.

"Yeah."

"We'll need to treat the studs before we put anything new up."

"I know."

Owen looked at the pile of removed drywall, then at the remaining panels, then at Steve. "We should do the upper ones tomorrow. These are easier when they're lower. Once we get the bottom row, the rest will be heavier."

"Right," Steve said.

Owen didn't respond to the praise. He just picked up the utility knife and checked the blade, snapping off the dull tip with his thumb and extending a fresh segment. He was already thinking about the next panel.

Then Doug arrived.

He came through the front entrance with his usual morning energy, flannel shirt tucked into jeans, hard hat tilted back on his white hair, a thermos in one hand and a paper bag in the other. He stopped when he saw the east wall. He stopped completely. The thermos lowered slowly to his side.

"Well," he said. "I'll be damned."

"Morning, Doug," Steve said.

Doug walked closer. He ran a hand along one of the exposed studs, then along the remaining drywall, then at the neat stack of removed panels against the far wall. His expression was unreadable. Steve couldn't tell if he was impressed or annoyed or both.

"You boys did all this?" Doug asked.

"Most of it," Owen said. "Just the lower panels so far."

Doug looked at Owen. He looked at the stack of panels. He looked back at Owen. "You pulled drywall?"

"Yes, sir."

"At your age?"

"I've done it before."

Doug's eyebrows rose. "When?"

"In middle school. My friend's dad was a contractor. I helped him once or twice."

Doug nodded slowly. He took a sip from his thermos and looked at the wall again, his expression shifting from surprise to something else—something that looked like respect, or maybe grief, or maybe both. Steve couldn't tell.

"Six panels," Doug said. "In a few hours. With the bottom row cut clean and stacked straight." He looked at Steve. "You taught him that?"

Steve opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "No," he said. "He figured it out."

Doug didn't respond. He just walked over to the stack of panels and ran his hand along the edge of the top one, checking the cut line with a craftsman's eye. His hand was steady. His face was calm. But Steve noticed Doug’s thumb tracing the drywall edge, over and over, a repetitive motion that felt like a silent apology.

Then Owen dropped the hammer.

It happened fast. Owen was reaching for the next panel, his right hand extended, his left hand supporting the bottom edge. The panel shifted. He adjusted his grip. His left hand slipped on the smooth face of the drywall, and he lurched forward, his right hand dropping the hammer as he tried to catch his balance.

The hammer fell. It hit the concrete and bounced, spinning end over end, and caught Owen on the side of the foot.

The sound was wrong. Not a thud. Not a crack. Something between them, a dull impact that Steve felt in his teeth more than he heard with his ears.

Owen went down.

Steve was moving before he thought about it. He crossed the distance in three strides, dropping to his knees beside his son, his hands already reaching, already assessing. Owen was clutching his left foot, his face contorted, his breath coming in short gasps that he was trying to suppress.

"Owen. Owen, look at me."

"I'm fine."

"Let me see."

"I'm fine, Dad."

Steve grabbed Owen's ankle and lifted his foot. The sneaker was scuffed, the toe cap dented inward. Owen's toes were curled tight inside the shoe. Steve could see the swelling already—rapid, localized, the kind of swelling that meant something was wrong beneath the surface.

"Okay," Steve said. "Okay, Doug. Don't move."

"It's just bruised."

"Owen."

Steve looked up. Doug was standing over them. His face was pale. His hands were clenched at his sides.

"Do you have ice?" Steve asked.

"In the truck," Doug said. "I'll get it."

"Thanks."

Doug turned and walked out. Steve knelt beside Owen, keeping his son's foot elevated on his own knee, applying gentle pressure above the ankle to slow the swelling. Owen was breathing harder now. The suppression was failing.

"Did it hit the bone?" Steve asked.

"I don't know."

"Can you wiggle your toes?"

Owen tried. His toes moved, but the movement was limited, stiff. He winced with each attempt.

"Okay," Steve said. "Ice first. Then we'll figure out the rest."

Owen closed his eyes. He pressed his lips together so tightly they went white. Steve recognized the expression—the same one Owen had worn when he'd broken his arm falling off the swing set at age seven, the same one he'd worn when he'd failed his driver's ed test and sat in the car in the driveway for twenty minutes without speaking. The expression of a boy who was trying very hard not to cry in front of his father.

Steve felt something break inside his chest. Not dramatically. Not with a sound. Just a quiet, internal fracture, the kind that happens when a man realizes he's been absent from something important and the realization comes too late to fix it.

Owen had been working. Owen had been helpful. Owen had been good at something Steve couldn't do, and Steve had been kneeling beside him, useless, watching his son try not to cry over a bruised foot while Steve's own hands shook and his mind raced with calculations that didn't matter.

The ice arrived. Doug knelt beside them, pressing the plastic bag against Owen's foot, wrapping it in a towel to prevent frostbite. Owen hissed through his teeth but didn't pull away.

"Better?" Steve asked.

"Less hot," Owen said.

Doug looked at Steve. "I'll take him home. Get it looked at properly."

Steve nodded. He stood up. His knees cracked. His back ached. He looked down at Owen, who was still on the floor, still holding his foot, still trying to be brave.

"I'm sorry," Steve said.

Owen looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed but dry. "It's okay, Dad. It's just a foot."

But it wasn't just a foot. Steve knew that now. It was everything—the work, the effort, the attempt, the failure, the injury, the apology that came too late. It was the architecture of everything he'd been trying to fix and couldn't.

He helped Owen stand. They walked slowly toward the front entrance, Doug carrying the ice bag and walking on Owen's other side, supporting his weight without making it obvious. Steve walked behind them, watching his son's face, watching Owen's jaw clench with each step, watching the silence that followed.

At the door, Owen stopped. He turned back to look at the store—at the east wall, at the exposed studs, at the stack of removed drywall panels stacked neatly against the far wall, at the orange mesh and the dust and the debris and the evidence of what they'd done and what they'd failed to do.

"We'll finish tomorrow," Owen said.

Steve didn't answer. He just nodded, the silence between them settling like dust after a collapse, heavy and final and enough.


# Chapter 6

He watched the tension in Owen’s jaw, the deliberate push of his throat as he forced the food down. # Chapter 13

The bell above the door chimed at ten-fifteen on a Saturday morning, a bright, brassy sound that cut through the low hum of the HVAC unit and the distant traffic outside the flagship store in Toledo. It was a cheerful sound. It was the kind of sound designed to make a customer feel welcome, to suggest that the space beyond the threshold was warm and stocked and ready to serve. Steve stood behind the counter, his hands flat on the laminate surface, and watched the door swing open.

He was wearing a shirt that wasn’t gray. It was a button-down, light blue, with the sleeves rolled to the elbow. It felt strange against his skin, tight across the shoulders, but he hadn’t taken it off. He hadn’t taken off the vest either—a navy pin-vest with the *Roberts & Co.* logo embroidered in white thread on the left breast. The embroidery was crooked. Owen had done it. Steve had held the fabric taut while his son fed the thread through the machine, his large, clumsy fingers fumbling with the tension dial, but the result was legible. It was proud. It was honest.

The store smelled different now. It didn’t smell like wet drywall anymore. It didn’t smell like ozone or pulverized limestone or the metallic tang of fear. It smelled of fresh paint—eggshell white, matte finish—and lemon oil. It smelled of new shelving units, galvanized steel and particleboard, stacked in neat rows along the north and south walls. The space was smaller. That was the first thing people noticed. The ceiling was lower, capped by the underside of the mezzanine that Doug had reinforced with steel plates and welds that scarred the metal like jagged seams. The east wall was gone, replaced by a large, floor-to-ceiling window that let in the morning light, turning the dust motes into suspended gold. The west wall was solid brick, painted white, lined with displays of hand tools, garden supplies, and the small, curated selection of hardware that Mei-Lin had arranged with such obsessive precision that every screw, every nut, every washer was grouped by size and material, labeled in a font that was clean and modern and entirely unlike the chaotic bin-system Steve had used for twenty years.

The memory came unbidden—the way memories of near-death tend to arrive: without invitation, fully formed, impossible to shake. Two weeks ago, same aisle. Doug had been on a ladder counting paint cans while Steve tallied fasteners below. The ceiling groaned—not a sound exactly, but a pressure, a deep muscular contraction in the bones of the building. Then the east wall buckled. A cascade of drywall and insulation sheeted between them like a drawn curtain, pinning Doug on one side, Steve on the other, trapped in the narrow aisle between shelves of garden hose and extension cords. Steve pressed his palms against the concrete, feeling the vibration travel through his ribs, listening to Doug's voice muffled behind a wall of debris, calling his name. Not angry. Not scared. Just certain. Certain that Steve wasn't going to make it through.

His palms rested flat against the workbench, steady as stone. The tremor had vanished without ceremony, dissolving like mist under a morning sun, leaving nothing but the familiar, calloused texture of his own skin. He curled his fingers inward. Sinew pulled taut beneath the surface. The tape measure weighed heavily in his pocket, its brass casing polished smooth by years of friction. He didn’t need it. He knew the dimensions of this counter by heart. He knew the distance to the door. He knew the exact spot where the floorboard groaned if you stepped on it with your left foot.

The customer was a woman in her sixties, wearing a beige coat and carrying a canvas tote bag. She stopped just inside the door, her eyes scanning the room. She looked at the shelves. She looked at the window. Then she looked at Steve. Her expression shifted—a flicker of recognition, quickly masked by a practiced neutrality. Steve recognized the mask. He'd worn it himself, in the mirror, every morning for the past week. The face you put on when you've been made public property and you're trying to pretend you're still a person.

But she didn’t look at his face. She looked at the display of hammers on the wall to his left. She walked toward it, her heels clicking softly on the polished concrete floor. Steve exhaled. He stepped out from behind the counter. He didn’t rush. He walked at a normal pace, deliberate and open-handed, not the guarded stride of a salesman, nor the shrinking gait of a victim.

The woman turned. She smiled, a small, polite smile that felt like a blanket draped over something fragile. "Good morning. I'm looking for a claw hammer. The 16-ounce. Do you carry Estwing?" She said it carefully, the way you might talk to a spooked animal. Not unkindly. But with a caution that told him she knew exactly what he was capable of unraveling.

Steve felt a surge of something—humiliation, pride, both so tightly wound he couldn't separate them. But he swallowed it down. He didn't let it show. He nodded toward the display. "Aisle three. Top shelf. We've got the direct drop forged. Red handle."

"Thank you," she said. She turned and walked away, but not before glancing back at him once, a quick, sympathetic flick of the eyes that confirmed what he already knew: the video had followed him home.

She thanked him, pivoted on her heel, and headed out.

Steve stood there for a moment, listening to the sound of her footsteps fading. He looked at Owen.

Owen was standing near the checkout register, which was a refurbished POS system that Mei-Lin had set up, complete with a barcode scanner and a receipt printer that chirped happily every time it printed. Owen was wearing a t-shirt and jeans, his hair messy, his pinky finger crooked as always. He was holding a tablet, scrolling through inventory numbers, but he had stopped scrolling. He was watching Steve. His expression was neutral, but his eyes were bright.

"That was easy," Owen said.

"It was," Steve said.

"She didn’t even know you," Owen said. "She didn’t care about the video. She just wanted a hammer."

"I know," Steve said.

That was the thing. The world hadn’t ended. The internet had moved on. The video of Steve breaking down in the parking lot had burned itself out, consumed by newer, louder disasters, and the people of Toledo had gone back to their lives, buying their hammers and their nails and their paint, and Steve Roberts had gone back to his store, smaller and quieter and real.

The door chimed again. This time it was Doug. He walked in wearing his old flannel shirt and a hard hat that he didn’t take off, even indoors. He carried a paper bag from the diner down the street, the kind of bag that leaked grease onto his fingers. He set it on the counter and looked around the store, his eyes lingering on the new window, the new shelves, the new sign above the door that read *Roberts & Co.* in letters that were bold and black and unapologetic.

"You did good," Doug said. He didn’t look at Steve. He looked at the ceiling. "Ceiling’s solid. I checked it myself. Those plates hold. I welded them myself. You didn’t have to worry about that."

"I didn't," Steve said.

Doug opened the bag and pulled out a coffee cup. He took a sip. He grimaced. "They put sugar in this. Like it’s a dessert. I hate it."

"It’s probably fine," Steve said.

"It’s sweet," Doug said. "Like a child. But not in a good way. In a annoying way." He paused. He looked at Steve. "You’re tired."

His gaze dropped to his palms, resting inert against his knees, while his eyelids weighed like lead curtains. The rest of him felt hewn from granite and dragged through grit. Four hours of sleep lay behind him, spent calibrating display lights, spacing bulbs with obsessive precision, angling beams to cast shadows just so. He did it because he could. Because he needed to command something, even if it was merely the trajectory of a single filament.

"I am," Steve said.

"Go home," Doug said. "Owen can handle the morning. I’ll stay. I like it here. It’s quiet. Quiet is good."

Steve’s gaze drifted toward the corner, catching Owen in the dim light. His son offered a loose shrug, a gesture of practiced ease. "I can handle it," he said. "I know where everything is."

Steve nodded. He didn’t argue. He didn’t give a speech. He just turned and walked toward the back office, the one that was now just a closet with a desk and a chair and a filing cabinet that was empty except for the original blueprints, the hand-drawn ones Doug had found, rolled up and tied with a string. He didn’t look at them. He didn’t need to. He knew what was in them. He knew the shape of the building. He knew the limits of its bones.

He stepped out into the November chill, under a slate-gray sky that seemed determined to withhold its snow. The wind bit at his face as he crossed the lot toward his Ford F-150, that rusted relic held together by duct tape and stubbornness. He slid behind the wheel, the seat vibrating against his spine as the engine caught, a familiar rhythm that grounded him before he pulled away from the curb.

He kept his phone in his pocket, a dead weight against his thigh, and watched the exhaust pipe shudder. Steam curled upward, dissolving into the gray Toledo morning, while the world around him continued its slow, indifferent rotation. His thoughts were anchored to the store, to the woman who needed a hammer and the man who needed to be useful. Doug stood behind the counter, nursing bad coffee and pretending to inspect the shelves, while Owen sat in the corner, scrolling through numbers with his pinky finger crooked, his eyes bright with a calculation Steve couldn’t quite read.

He thought about Marla. She was still in Columbus. She had sent a text two days ago—*“I’m coming home this weekend. Don’t cook.”*—and he had stared at it for a long time, his thumb hovering over the screen, unsure of what to type. He hadn’t typed anything. He didn’t know what to say. He didn’t know if he had earned the right to speak to her yet. But he knew she was coming. And that was enough.

He let the silence wrap around him, heavy and warm and familiar, and for the first time in months, he didn’t try to fill it with numbers. The engine hummed a low, steady note against the chassis, a vibration he could feel in his teeth rather than hear, grounding him in the physical reality of the truck and the dust-choked air. He didn’t need to calculate the load or measure the distance to know he was exactly where he needed to be.


# Chapter 14

The conference room at the Torrid Home regional office smelled of expensive leather and cold air conditioning, a scent that Steve had come to associate with the precise moment his life stopped making sense. He sat at the far end of a table that cost more than his first car, his hands folded on the polished surface to keep them from trembling. Across from him sat Piper Bellamy, twenty-nine years old, wearing a blazer that fit too perfectly and a smile that looked like it had been calibrated by the same algorithms she presumably used to optimize inventory turnover.

She tapped a tablet screen. The sound was sharp, like a bone breaking.

"Steve," she said. "Let's talk about liquidation."

He didn't look at her. He looked at the window behind her, which overlooked the Columbus skyline. It was gray out there. Everything was gray lately. The sky, the pavement, the email notifications that pinged in his pocket with increasing desperation. He kept checking them. Fourteen times a day. He couldn't stop. It was a tic, like blinking, like breathing.

"Liquidation," he repeated. The word tasted like copper.

"Torrid Home doesn't have the capital to fund a full rebuild of a Class-B big-box structure in the Midwest," Piper said. Her voice was upbeat, algorithmic, the voice of someone who had never had to explain why a roof had fallen on her father. "The insurance payout covers the demolition and the debris removal. That's it. There's no contingency fund for reconstruction. Not for this size of asset."

Steve felt a coldness spread through his chest, starting at his sternum and moving outward to his fingertips. He willed his hands to stay still. He thought about the store. The way the light used to hit the dust motes in aisle four in the late afternoon, creating this golden haze that made the screws and nails and washers look like treasure. He thought about Doug, sixty-two years old, who had been stocking shelves since 1981, who knew the weight of every box and the price of every bolt. He thought about Owen, who had filmed him crying in the parking lot, who was probably watching the video right now, laughing or crying or doing something worse—something indifferent.

"You're telling me," Steve said, his voice sounding thin and distant, "that you're going to let the building sit there. Broken. Until it falls down completely?"

"We're telling you," Piper corrected gently, "that the building is a total loss. And that you have a choice. You can take the insurance money, sign over the land to the developer—there's a bid on the table already, residential mixed-use—and walk away with enough to retire comfortably. Or you can try to rebuild it yourself, with your own money, which you don't have, and hope that Torrid Home changes its mind about funding a project that has a negative ROI before it even breaks ground."

She paused. She let the silence hang in the air, heavy and suffocating. Steve could hear the hum of the refrigerator in the corner, the low thrum of the HVAC system, the sound of his own heart beating against his ribs. He counted the beats. One. Two. Three. He stopped when he reached twelve.

"And the third option?" he asked.

"There is no third option, Steve."

"Yes, there is." He looked up at her then. Really looked at her. He saw the slight tremor in her left hand, the way her eyes darted to the door every few seconds, as if she were expecting someone to burst in and tell her she wasn't allowed to be there. He saw the vulnerability she was trying to hide behind the blazer and the tablet and the confident posture. She was scared. She was terrified. And she was trying to prove that her methodology worked, that the numbers could save her, even when the numbers said everyone should die.

"What's the third option?" he asked again.

Piper sighed. It was a small sound, almost imperceptible. "You can fight it. You can hire a lawyer. You can appeal the assessment. But the assessment is based on structural engineering reports, Steve. The roof is gone. The walls are compromised. The electrical system is fried. The numbers are correct. They're not wrong. They're just… cruel."

Steve stood up. His legs felt shaky, but he forced them to hold him. He walked to the window and looked out at the gray city. He thought about the store. He thought about the load-bearing walls, the ones his dad had built with his own hands, the ones that had held up the roof for forty years. He thought about the way the building had exhaled when the roof collapsed, like a beast curling into sleep. He thought about the silence that followed.

"I'm not signing anything," he said.

"Steve," Piper said, her voice softening. "Please. Don't make this harder than it has to be. You're grieving. I get it. But grief doesn't pay contractors."

Steve turned to face her. He felt a sudden surge of anger, hot and bright and terrifying. He wanted to scream. He wanted to throw the tablet through the window. He wanted to grab her by the shoulders and shake her until she admitted that the numbers were wrong, that there was a way to fix things, that if you just tried hard enough, you could put the pieces back together.

But he didn't. He just stood there, breathing hard, his hands clenched into fists at his sides.

"You're right," he said finally. "The numbers are correct."

Piper exhaled. Relief washed over her face, visible and immediate.

"But," Steve continued, "they don't matter."

"What?"

"The numbers. They don't matter. Because I'm not doing this for the numbers. I'm doing this because it's mine. Because my dad built it. Because my son needs to see me try. Because…" He trailed off. He couldn't say it. He couldn't say *I didn't mean to lose everything*. He couldn't say *I'm afraid I'm already gone*.

"Because what?" Piper asked.

Steve looked down at his hands. They were still shaking. He put them in his pockets.

"Nothing," he said. "Just… leave me alone."

He walked out of the conference room. He didn't look back. He walked down the hallway, past the glass walls and the minimalist decor and the smell of expensive leather, and he stepped into the elevator. The doors closed behind him. The elevator descended. Steve leaned his head against the cool metal wall and closed his eyes.

He thought about the store. He thought about the roof. He thought about the way the light used to hit the dust motes in aisle four. He thought about Doug. He thought about Owen.

And then he thought about the third option. The one Piper hadn't mentioned. The one that didn't have a name. The one that required him to do something he hadn't done since he was a kid helping his dad in the garage: to build something with his own hands. To fix something broken. To see if it could hold weight again.

The elevator dinged. The doors opened. Steve stepped out into the lobby. He walked out the front door and into the gray afternoon. He walked to his truck. He got in. He started the engine. He sat there for a long time, listening to the idle, watching the rain begin to fall.

Then he drove home.
